### Lineage

Rite Tec draws on seven influences; each contributes one job, which is the justification for including it.

| Influence | Lineage | What Rite Tec takes | What it leaves |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Popping and boogaloo | Credited to Boogaloo Sam, who founded the Electric Boogaloos in Fresno in 1977; origin accounts are contested | Hits, waves, robot (botting), miming, dime stops, all part of the popping toolkit | Funk groove and boogaloo leg rolls |
| Hip-hop cypher | Circle-based freestyle culture | The format: improvised, call-and-response, dancer-centered | Battle aggression as the default mood |
| Aerobics (Gilad Janklowicz) | Gilad Janklowicz (1954 to 2026), whose Bodies in Motion ran 42 years and about 600 episodes | Aerobic patterns and moves, endurance, rhythm, breath; directional travel carried by house dance legwork | Platform, choreography-for-workout logic |
| Industrial, cybergoth and Tecktonik | Cybergoth blends industrial aesthetics with gothic raver style; industrial dance as a goth style developed in the mid-to-late 2000s, spreading via YouTube | Aesthetic, music world, stomps, one move per beat; diagonal and crossing arm patterns and tectonic moves, taken further into complex, martial-looking quick hand work | Pure repetition without technique depth |
| Chinese martial arts (Taiji, I Liq Chuan, Wing Chun) | Taiji (Chen Silk Reeling, Yang stances). I Liq Chuan, founded by Chin Lik Keong and carried on by Sam Chin, an internal art based on Chan and Taiji principles with roots in rare Xingyi and Bagua styles. Wing Chun, a southern style built on centerline and stance structure, usually not classed as internal. | Spiral routing, rooting, empty and full, bone alignment, centerline, turning stance, awareness | Combat application, qi theory as a claim |
| Contemporary and theatrical jazz | Modern dance (Graham contraction and release, Humphrey fall and recovery) and theatrical jazz (Jack Cole, Bob Fosse) | Contractions, falls, reaches: dramatic peaks, level changes, emotional weight | Narrative acting, facial emoting, continuous lyrical flow |
| Butoh | Japanese avant-garde dance founded by Tatsumi Hijikata and Kazuo Ohno; the first piece, Kinjiki, premiered in 1959 | Dissonance as technique, mask changes, slow drift, sudden violent bursts, transformation | Full white body paint as default; techno and dark styling instead, white masks as an option |

**What is new in Rite Tec:** the parts exist separately; the claim is the integration. Popping usually isolates; Neijia usually connects. Rite Tec uses connection to *power* the isolation: the hit is a visible endpoint of a whole-body spiral.


### Music

**Rite Tec covers the witch house to techno perimeter, plus experimental electronic and IDM, and each genre pulls different movement to the front.** Tempos below are approximate genre norms, not rules.

| Genre | Typical feel | Foregrounded movement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Witch house | Slow, half-time, heavy and occult | Spirals, planes, slow drift, masks, gliding steps |
| Darkwave | Mid-tempo, melodic, cold | Spirals into stops, centerline pivots |
| Electro | Broken, robotic, around 125 to 135 BPM | Hits, stops, robot angles |
| EBM and industrial | Four-on-the-floor, around 140 BPM for industrial dance club tracks | Rooting, stomps, advancing steps |
| Techno | Driving, hypnotic, roughly 125 to 145 BPM | The stepping engine, long trance phrases |
| Experimental electronic and IDM | Irregular, glitchy, shifting meters and textures | Hits and stops on micro-rhythms, slow drift, masks |
| Darkgaze | Reverb-drowned synth walls, drone and black-metal atmosphere, slow build | Slow drift, masks, spirals, planes |
| Hard synth and electropunk | Distorted, aggressive synths, punk energy, raw and chaotic | Hits, quick hand work, stops, stomps, masks at full intensity |

**Aesthetic:** techno and dark styling first. The face ranges from neutral to intensely emotional to rapidly switching masks; white-painted Butoh masks are an occasional option, expressing more of a gothic / black metal aesthetics. The fog is part of the staging: the figure appears and disappears in light, and the precision of the stops is what cuts through it.
