### Mechanics

#### Main principles

- **Power from the ground.** Every hit starts with a press of the foot into the floor. Nothing is done by the arm alone.
- **The spiral.** Force turns through the waist and spine on its way up, as in Taiji Silk Reeling. Transitions are spirals, not straight lines.
- **Load and release.** Tension builds in the spiral and lets go at the hand, as in Fa Jin. Staying soft between hits is what makes the hits read as hard.
- **Empty and full.** One leg carries the weight at a time. Every step has a clear base, which keeps fast footwork clean.
- **Centerline.** From Wing Chun: bones stack from foot to hand along the body's aim. Stops look locked, not tense.
- **Awareness.** From I Liq Chuan: freestyle as listening. I answer the track in the moment instead of imposing set material.

#### Bone and fascia

- **Bones as struts, fascia as cables.** Biotensegrity, a term coined by orthopedic surgeon Stephen Levin, describes bones as compression elements held in a continuous tension network of fascia, muscle and ligament. It is a useful model in bodywork and movement education, still debated in mainstream biomechanics. For Rite Tec it explains why a hit in the hand can be driven from the foot: tension carries across the whole network.
- **The fascial catapult.** Kangaroos jump farther than their muscle force explains because leg tendons and fascia are tensioned like elastic bands and then released. Humans use the same mechanism. The rebound in the legs and the hit at the hand are both catapult actions: preload, then release.
- **Spiral routing.** Thomas Myers' Anatomy Trains maps continuous myofascial lines; the Spiral Line wraps diagonally around the trunk and is the anatomical counterpart of Silk Reeling. Rite Tec's spiral transitions train this line deliberately.
- **Practical consequence:** Rite Tec favors springy, oscillating repetitions over grinding effort, which is also how fascia-oriented training builds elastic storage capacity.


### Space

**Rule: straight lines for the signal, spirals for the transmission, verticals for the engine.** Each layer owns a geometry, which is what makes the style read as precise rather than busy.

- **Six directions.** The oldest sense of liu he (six harmonies) is the six directions of space: forward and back, left and right, up and down. Rite Tec uses them as its base grid: every stop and plane resolves onto one of these axes.
- **Dimensional vs diagonal.** In Laban's space harmony, the three dimensions cross at the body's center of gravity and form an octahedron; diagonals run to the corners of a cube. Robot, mime and popping live on dimensional lines; spirals and crossing hand work travel diagonals. Switching between the two is a core compositional tool.
- **Kinesphere and reach.** Laban defines the kinesphere as the space limbs can reach without changing place, with near, mid and far reach. Rite Tec phrases often compress to near reach (hands at the chest, tight planes) then snap to far reach on the drop.
- **Centerline facing.** From Wing Chun: the arms align to a centerline pointing where the chest points, and Chum Kiu trains turning that aligned unit toward a moving target. In Rite Tec the centerline is the body's aim: turns rotate it in clear steps, so 360-degree club dancing stays legible from any side.
- **Sphere of awareness.** I Liq Chuan calls itself the martial art of awareness. Rite Tec borrows the attitude: the kinesphere is a sphere the dancer monitors in all six directions, not a stage with one front.
